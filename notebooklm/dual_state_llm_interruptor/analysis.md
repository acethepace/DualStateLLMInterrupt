Unlocking Full-Duplex AI: A Comprehensive Analysis of Interruption Tokens and Streaming Dynamics in Large Language ModelsIntroduction to Full-Duplex Spoken Dialogue SystemsThe evolution of large language models (LLMs) has profoundly reshaped human-computer interaction, yet the realization of seamless, human-like conversation relies heavily on overcoming the limitations of traditional turn-taking paradigms. Historically, spoken dialogue models (SDMs) have operated in a half-duplex configuration, enforcing a rigid, sequential structure where one participant remains entirely idle while the other generates a response. In these legacy turn-based systems, user interruptions must typically be triggered by explicit, out-of-band mechanisms—such as a manual "stop" button, a predefined wake word, or an extended period of absolute acoustic silence—resulting in interactions that are conspicuously artificial and operationally fragile.The next frontier in computational linguistics, embodied artificial intelligence, and multi-agent dialogue systems is the full-duplex conversational model. Full-duplex systems are designed to listen and speak simultaneously, mimicking the biological and cognitive processes of human conversation where participants continuously process incoming acoustic or textual information, formulate responses, and navigate overlapping speech. This paradigm introduces complex requirements for real-time coordination between listening, speaking, and "thinking." It necessitates advanced dialogue management to handle backchanneling, varying network latencies, and user barge-ins without losing the semantic thread of the conversation.Achieving true full-duplex interaction dictates that a model must be capable of processing streaming input and dynamically adjusting its behavior, specifically by generating explicit interruption tokens to seize or yield the conversational floor. This report comprehensively examines the current research landscape concerning LLM interruption mechanisms. It analyzes the architectural adaptations required for token-based interruption, semantic voice activity detection (VAD) strategies, cross-attention routing frameworks, synchronous wall-clock alignments, multi-agent protocol routing, and the underlying hardware scheduling techniques (such as chunked prefill) required to facilitate natural interruption in both human-to-agent and agent-to-agent environments.Token-Based Interruption and Semantic Voice Activity DetectionA central challenge in full-duplex systems is distinguishing between varying types of overlapping speech. Human conversations are replete with unintentional interruptions, hesitations, and backchannels (e.g., "mm-hmm," "yeah," "exactly") that signify active listening and cognitive alignment rather than an attempt to claim the conversational floor. Traditional acoustic Voice Activity Detection (VAD) models rely solely on the presence of sound. This acoustic-only methodology leads to frequent conversational breakdowns, as the agent's turn is prematurely terminated when a user issues a simple backchannel or pauses briefly to gather their thoughts, leading to an erratic and frustrating user experience.To resolve this bottleneck, recent research has introduced Semantic VAD, which operates as an intelligent, lightweight dialogue manager (DM) to regulate turn-taking based on lexical, syntactic, and contextual comprehension rather than mere acoustic energy. By fine-tuning a small-scale LLM (e.g., a 0.5B parameter model) on carefully curated full-duplex conversation data, the Semantic VAD processes input speech in ultra-short intervals and predicts specific control tokens to guide the heavier Core Dialogue Engine (CDE).Control Tokens for Micro-Dialogue ManagementThe Semantic VAD utilizes a discrete set of action tokens to manage the conversational state dynamically. These tokens allow the system to differentiate between intentional barge-ins (where the user wishes to actively redirect the conversation, correct the agent, or introduce a new query) and unintentional interruptions (such as background noise, ambient conversational bleed, or acknowledgments).Control TokenSystem ConditionSystem Action and Implication<|Start-Speaking|> (<|S-S|>)Query CompleteIndicates the user has finished their semantic thought. This decoupled architecture balances interaction accuracy with inference efficiency. It allows the system to scale economically without continuously running the primary text-generation LLM (which may be tens or hundreds of billions of parameters) during the entirety of the user's speech.Time-Division Multiplexing and Wall-Clock SynchronizationWhile decoupling the dialogue manager from the core engine is highly efficient, an alternative philosophical approach is the holistic conversion of an autoregressive LLM into a unified "duplex model." Standard autoregressive models face substantial structural barriers to duplex operation, as they are inherently designed to process an entire input sequence before emitting the first output token, fundamentally cementing them into a turn-based paradigm.To overcome this, researchers have adapted existing foundational LLMs using a Time-Division-Multiplexing (TDM) encoding-decoding strategy. In the TDM framework, both user queries and agent responses are segmented into discrete time slices (e.g., representing $k$ seconds of audio converted to text or direct acoustic tokens). The model processes these alternating slices pseudo-simultaneously, treating the dialogue as a continuous, interleaved stream rather than discrete message blocks.During training on specialized datasets like Duplex-UltraChat, these LLMs are exposed to varied interactive scenarios, including generation termination, rapid regeneration, and dialogue resets triggered by specific user inputs mid-stream. Rather than waiting for an explicit end-of-turn token, the duplex model evaluates the context after every time slice. If the model determines it should not yet respond—perhaps because the user is still formulating a thought—it outputs a specialized idle token (e.g., <wait>), indicating its decision to remain silent and await further user input. If the idle token is omitted, the model's generated slice is immediately delivered to the user. This configuration closely emulates human cognitive processing, allowing the LLM to effectively "think while listening" and "speak while observing".Synchronous LLMs (SyncLLM) and Predict-Ahead MaskingExpanding on the concept of temporal slicing, the SyncLLM architecture folds objective wall-clock timing directly into the model's operational matrix. SyncLLM turns a standard autoregressive model into a wall-clock-synchronized agent that operates in fixed 160-to-240-millisecond chunks. Speech is represented not as text, but as discrete HuBERT units operating at approximately 25 Hz (translating to roughly 40 ms per token), supplemented with periodic speaker tags to maintain temporal alignment.A critical vulnerability in real-time, network-based full-duplex systems is packet loss and internet jitter, which can delay the arrival of the user's audio chunks, causing the agent to stall or hallucinate interruptions. SyncLLM mitigates this through predict-ahead masking. At each chunk $N$, if the user's chunk has not yet arrived due to network delay, the model actively predicts the user's missing chunk to mask the latency. It then generates its own chunk $N+1$. If the user's actual audio subsequently arrives and contradicts the prediction, the model seamlessly overwrites its prediction, enabling robust out-of-distribution generalization and preserving conversational fluidity even under 200 ms of internet jitter.Internal Architectural Routing for Simultaneous StreamsFor a unified LLM to successfully issue an interruption token during continuous speech, it must possess an architectural pathway to process the incoming user stream while concurrently maintaining its own generation context. As established, standard LLMs are designed to extend a single, coherent sequence, making the simultaneous ingestion of a new stream a fundamental architectural hurdle.Recent research dissects this stream-routing problem into two primary paradigms: Channel Fusion (CF) and Cross-Attention (XA) routing. Furthermore, exploring how models like the Listening-while-Speaking Language Model (LSLM) fuse these channels provides critical insight into the future of unified duplex architectures.Channel Fusion (CF-Duplex)In Channel Fusion architectures, the incoming user stream is injected directly into the LLM's primary input sequence alongside the model's own generated tokens. This unified sequence utilizes explicit special tokens to denote the origin of the audio or text data, effectively interleaving the streams into a single timeline.The primary strength of Channel Fusion lies in its deep semantic grounding. By forcing the LLM to process the user's input through the exact same self-attention mechanisms as its own output, the model exhibits superior performance in complex reasoning and spoken question-answering tasks. The model can intimately associate its current utterance with the user's immediate interjection.However, CF architectures are highly vulnerable to context corruption during overlapping speech. If a user interrupts the model, and the model fails to emit a stop or yield token immediately, the competing streams mix inextricably within the self-attention window. This often leads to semantically incoherent, hallucinated continuations, as the model attempts to synthesize a response that aligns with both its ongoing (but now irrelevant) sentence and the contradictory user interruption.Cross-Attention Routing (XA-Duplex)Conversely, in Cross-Attention routing, the user stream is isolated as an external memory bank. The primary LLM accesses this stream exclusively through specialized cross-attention adapters, keeping the primary autoregressive generation sequence free of external user tokens.This architecture inherently preserves the LLM's generation context. During a user interruption, even if the model's reaction time is delayed and it does not immediately issue an interruption token, its ongoing generation remains semantically coherent because the user's speech does not overwrite or corrupt the primary sequence. The trade-off, however, is that XA models generally underperform CF models in rigorous question-answering benchmarks, as the separation of streams slightly degrades the model's ability to deeply and instantly integrate the semantic nuances of the user's prompt into its immediate reasoning process.Fusion Strategies in LSLM and FD-SLMThe timing of when these streams are fused is equally critical. The Listening-while-Speaking Language Model (LSLM) employs a token-based, decoder-only Text-to-Speech (TTS) system coupled with a streaming Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) encoder for real-time audio input. Researchers exploring early fusion, middle fusion, and late fusion strategies have found that middle fusion achieves the optimal balance between speech generation quality and real-time interaction responsiveness.Furthermore, probing the hidden representations of Full-Duplex Spoken Language Models (FD-SLMs) reveals stream-specific predictive biases: during active listening, the model's internal states preferentially predict the incoming user stream, whereas during speaking, they preferentially predict the model's own output stream. Understanding and manipulating these latent predictive biases is crucial for tuning a model's sensitivity to interruption tokens.Chronological Thinking and Latent Logic EncodingA persistent issue in full-duplex generation is the idle state of the model while the user is speaking. Many early full-duplex SDMs handled the user's turn by forcing the agent to repeatedly emit "silence tokens." However, forcing an autoregressive model to repeatedly predict silence severely biases the next-token probability distribution, leading to repetitive degeneration, semantic drift, and a total waste of the computational window that humans typically use for lightweight reasoning.To mitigate this computational waste, the Chronological Thinking (CT-Duplex) framework replaces silence tokens with "thinking nodes," a concept inspired by the Adaptive Control of Thought-Rational (ACT-R) theoretical framework. As the user's streaming speech is ingested, these nodes are chronologically predicted based on real-time semantic segments, ensuring causality in the reasoning process.In traditional architectures, the prefill stage exclusively encodes the input history into the Key-Value (KV) cache. In the CT-Duplex model, the decode stage assumes a dual role during the user's speech: it models the probability distribution of the next semantic thought while simultaneously encoding the current audio token into the cache. By executing these chronological thinking chains during the listening phase, the model significantly improves its reasoning quality upon being prompted to speak, demonstrating an 8.75% performance improvement on complex benchmarks like SpokenWOZ. This methodology decouples audio prediction from the core LLM text backbone, proving that utilizing the "listening window" productively enhances the agent's ability to interrupt with high-value, logically sound contributions rather than mere reactive conversational fillers.Multi-Agent Interruption and Communication EfficiencyWhile human-to-agent interruption focuses heavily on naturalness, acoustic handling, and latency, agent-to-agent interruption addresses profound challenges in computational efficiency, API cost management, and context window saturation. Multi-agent systems utilizing LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in complex reasoning, distributed software engineering, and simulated social environments. However, inter-agent communication frequently suffers from highly verbose outputs, which overload shared context windows, drastically increase computational costs, and delay multi-agent consensus.Traditionally, multi-agent frameworks optimize communication by compressing the message on the speaker's side before transmission. Yet, speaker-oriented compression is inherently flawed; the speaker cannot accurately predict what information the listener already possesses, what private context the listener holds, or what specific clarifications the listener requires to execute its designated function.The HANDRAISER FrameworkTo resolve this inefficiency, the HANDRAISER framework introduces a highly effective interruptible communication protocol specifically tailored for multi-agent LLM ecosystems. Mirroring human cognitive economy, HANDRAISER empowers the listening agent to actively halt the speaker once the listener has received sufficient information to proceed with its task, thereby bypassing redundant data streams.In this architecture, the speaking agent transmits a stream of tokens in fixed-size segments (chunks) $c_i^t$. Upon receiving each chunk, the listening agent evaluates its current comprehension against its task requirements. If the listener decides it has enough context, it transmits an explicit interruption token to the speaker, halting the speaker's generation pipeline, and immediately begins synthesizing its own response or executing its programmatic action.Prompt-based implementations of this system initially revealed that foundational LLMs are often overly confident, interrupting prematurely before acquiring critical task-specific information, leading to catastrophic task failures. To properly calibrate the interruption timing, HANDRAISER employs a supervised learning methodology predicated on tree sampling. The system samples multiple multi-agent rollouts and estimates the future payoff of interrupting at various specific token intervals.The payoff is defined by two competing metrics:Reward (Task Performance): Does the interruption degrade the final task success rate or consensus quality?Cost (Token Reduction): How many generation tokens (and associated compute costs) are saved by halting the speaker early?During training, the model uses the binary symbol of the payoff (positive or negative) as a training signal. If an interruption point yields lower token costs without degrading the final task performance, it is labeled as a successful interruption point.Empirical evaluations across diverse multi-agent scenarios—such as 2-agent text Pictionary games, 3-agent meeting scheduling protocols, and 3-agent formal debates—demonstrate significant efficiency gains. The HANDRAISER protocol reduces overall communication costs by an average of 32.2% (24.3% in Pictionary, 23.4% in meeting scheduling, and a staggering 48.9% in debate scenarios) while maintaining or explicitly exceeding baseline task performance. This provides definitive proof that listener-initiated interruption tokens are vastly more efficient than speaker-side summarization algorithms.Protocol Routing and Ambient Agent OrchestrationThe efficacy of inter-agent interruptions is heavily dependent on the underlying transport layer and communication protocols. As large-scale multi-agent systems evolve from research prototypes to production enterprise applications, the protocol layer becomes a critical bottleneck.ProtocolBench and ProtocolRouterAs highlighted by ProtocolBench—a systematic evaluation framework for agent protocols—the choice of communication protocols (e.g., A2A, ACP, Agora, ANP) significantly dictates end-to-end latency, byte overhead, and robustness during failure states. In streaming queue scenarios where continuous data flow is required, protocols like ACP achieve much lower mean latencies (e.g., 9.66 seconds) compared to others like Agora (13.14 seconds), resulting in a variance of up to 36.5% in total task completion times.To optimize this, dynamic deployment schemas like ProtocolRouter evaluate the specific multi-agent context and route communication through the most efficient protocol, reducing fail-storm recovery times by up to 18.1%. Implementing interruption tokens in these arrays requires robust, low-latency transport layers that can handle asynchronous state changes without tearing down and restarting the entire execution workflow.Durable Execution and Ambient InterruptionIn traditional microservice architectures, failing or interrupting a process means retrying from the top. In LLM ecosystems, retrying a massive data pipeline due to a simple agent interruption can cost thousands of dollars in wasted compute. Systems like Redpanda and Temporal utilize "durable execution" to allow workflows to survive interruptions and resume exactly where they left off.For example, in financial multi-agent systems, "ambient agents" must operate continuously without explicit human prompts. Using Temporal schedules, developers can initiate an ensemble-nudge workflow that triggers proactive analysis on a set interval (e.g., every 25 seconds). If a human overseer or another agent interrupts the workflow via a direct Signal, the agent pauses, ingests the new parameters, and resumes the workflow without dropping its historical context or requiring a hard reboot.Hardware and Systems Infrastructure for Streaming PrefillThe theoretical architecture for interruption tokens is useless if the underlying hardware inference engine cannot process the tokens in real-time. Full-duplex systems demand sub-500 millisecond latency to feel natural to human operators. However, LLM inference is traditionally divided into two distinct computational phases, which are structurally hostile to continuous, low-latency streaming.Prefill Phase: The model processes all input tokens simultaneously, computes their attention states, and populates the Key-Value (KV) cache. Prefill evaluates many tokens in parallel and is heavily compute-bound.Decode Phase: The model generates output one token at a time by reading the previously cached KV states. Decode operations rely heavily on memory bandwidth rather than raw computation, as the entire KV cache must be read for each sequentially generated token.The Necessity of Chunked PrefillIn a standard, unoptimized deployment, a large incoming user prompt (e.g., a lengthy streaming utterance, a massive injected context window, or a retrieved document) initiates a massive prefill operation. Because prefill monopolizes the GPU's compute resources, all ongoing decode operations for that specific model instance are paused until the prefill completes. If a duplex agent is listening to a user while trying to generate an interruption token, but the system suddenly receives a massive chunk of text, the agent's decode phase freezes. This inter-token latency spike completely destroys the illusion of a responsive, full-duplex agent.To solve this, modern inference engines utilize Chunked Prefill (also referred to as incremental prefill). Chunked prefill is an advanced scheduling technique that divides a long input prompt into smaller, manageable token budgets. Instead of processing the entire prompt in one uninterrupted block, the scheduler interleaves active decode steps with small chunks of the prefill.The mechanism operates by assigning a fixed token budget per iteration. It prioritizes the ongoing decode operations (ensuring continuous token generation for the user), and uses the remaining budget to compute a chunk of the prefill. The resulting KV pairs are cached iteratively. Advanced frameworks like TensorRT-LLM deploy dynamic chunk sizing, which automatically determines the ideal activation buffer sizes based on real-time GPU utilization metrics, eliminating manual configuration and preventing memory fragmentation.By preventing long prefill requests from monopolizing the GPU, chunked prefill guarantees that inter-token latency remains consistently low. This allows a full-duplex agent to instantly emit a <|Start-Listening|> interruption token exactly when required, regardless of concurrent background processing.Disaggregated Prefill and Decode via RDMAFor enterprise-scale multi-agent systems, even chunked prefill may not suffice to hit strict Time-to-First-Token (TTFT) and Tokens-Per-Second (TPS) targets simultaneously, as it requires duplicating the model across execution pools. Researchers have pioneered Disaggregated Serving, physically separating the prefill and decode phases onto different network nodes.In a disaggregated architecture, dedicated "prefiller nodes" populate the KV caches and transfer them over a high-speed network—typically using Remote Direct Memory Access (RDMA) primitives coordinated by libraries like libfabric—to dedicated "decoder nodes". To minimize latency, these KV page copies are initiated the moment the prefiller appends cache entries for individual model layers, rather than waiting for the entire forward pass to complete.Because the decoders never have to halt their text generation to process a compute-heavy prefill, decode latency becomes highly deterministic. This infrastructure is the ultimate enabler for massive, responsive multi-agent ecosystems where rapid, token-level interruption is mandatory for systemic stability.Task-Aware KV Cache PruningEven with chunked prefill and disaggregation, maintaining the full KV cache for long interactions is highly resource-intensive. Emerging techniques like CompactAttention, QUOKA, SnapKV, and Quest address this by performing query-dependent KV selection and task-aware KV cache pruning during the chunked prefill phase. By actively discarding KV entries deemed unimportant for future decoding (using recent query tokens as evaluators), these systems drastically reduce the memory footprint of the agent, ensuring that the system can sustain rapid interruption and response cycles indefinitely without suffering from out-of-memory (OOM) fatal errors.Simultaneous Generation and the Wait-$k$ PolicyThe mathematical foundation for determining when an LLM should interrupt or begin speaking heavily borrows from Simultaneous Machine Translation (SiMT) research. SiMT requires a model to generate translations incrementally as the source sentence is actively being streamed, demanding a strict read/write policy to balance translation accuracy against latency.The Wait-$k$ FrameworkThe most prominent heuristic policy in streaming translation is the wait-$k$ strategy. A wait-$k$ decoder first reads $k$ source tokens to establish a preliminary semantic context. Following this initial buffer, the model alternates strictly between producing one target token and reading one new source token.Mathematically, the wait-$k$ policy dictates the length of the partial input sequence $g_i$ required when generating the target token $y_i$. For a source sequence of length $J$, the policy is defined as:$$g_i = \min\{k + i - 1, J\}$$where the model reads up to $k + i - 1$ tokens before outputting the $i$-th token. While wait-$k$ is computationally simple and highly efficient in low-resource environments, it is entirely rigid. It cannot dynamically adapt to the semantic completeness of the incoming stream, meaning it will blindly wait for tokens even if the linguistic intent is already perfectly clear.LLM-Driven Simultaneous Generation (LSG)To transcend rigid heuristic algorithms, researchers have developed dynamic, LLM-driven policies. The LLM-driven Simultaneous Generation (LSG) framework elevates the LLM itself to the role of the read/write policy-maker without requiring complex dynamic programming or external routing modules.In LSG, the system utilizes a minimal-latency baseline policy (such as wait-$1$). At each step, the LLM evaluates the probability distribution of the next target word using both the complete current input and the limited input defined by the baseline policy. If the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between these two probability distributions falls below a predefined threshold—indicating that the LLM is highly confident in its prediction despite lacking future context—the model selects the WRITE action and generates the token.If the divergence is high, indicating semantic uncertainty, the model issues a READ action, suppressing generation and waiting for additional streaming input. This confidence-conditioned generation allows off-the-shelf LLMs to achieve state-of-the-art simultaneous generation, seamlessly transitioning between listening and interrupting based strictly on internal predictive confidence rather than arbitrary timing rules.Translation by Anticipating Future (TAF) and Monotonic AttentionPushing the boundary of predictive interruption further, the Translation by Anticipating Future (TAF) policy uses the LLM to actively hallucinate the future of the user's sentence. Given a partial streaming prefix, the LLM generates multiple probabilistic continuations of the user's speech. The system then translates each hallucinated continuation and employs a majority-voting mechanism to select the most agreed-upon prefix. By accurately anticipating the user's intent, the model can safely interrupt or begin speaking with extremely low latency—often reducing the wait time to just 3 words while improving translation quality by up to 5 BLEU scores.Furthermore, applying these predictive models to raw audio introduces challenges with non-monotonic alignments (where the order of words in one language does not match another). Innovations like the MonoAttn-Transducer integrate a learnable monotonic attention mechanism using a forward-backward algorithm to infer posterior probabilities of alignments. This allows transducer models to adaptively adjust their scope of attention during streaming generation without enumerating the exponentially large alignment space, vastly improving the timing of audio-based interruption tokens.Benchmarking Interruption and Conversational DynamicsAs full-duplex architectures stabilize into production-ready deployments, the academic focus has shifted toward rigorously evaluating their naturalness, seamlessness, and intelligence. Traditional metrics like BLEU or Word Error Rate (WER) are wildly insufficient for assessing temporal conversational dynamics, as they completely ignore timing, pacing, and interruption handling. A new suite of benchmarks has emerged to explicitly measure turn-taking and interruption efficacy.FLEXI and Full-Duplex-BenchFLEXI is the first benchmark designed to evaluate full-duplex LLM-human spoken interaction with a specific emphasis on model interruption during highly stressful emergency scenarios. The benchmark categorizes duplex interactions into three tiers, where Level 1 mirrors face-to-face human conversation, demanding latencies strictly below 150 ms. FLEXI tests whether a model can produce a silence response within $k$ tokens of a user interruption, revealing significant, critical gaps in open-source and commercial models regarding emergency awareness and turn termination.Full-Duplex-Bench (FDB-v1 and FDB-v2) provides a fully automated framework for evaluating streaming interactions across Daily, Correction, Entity Tracking, and Safety task families. FDB-v2 introduces an LLM-based "Examiner" agent that interacts with the "Evaluatee" system in real-time. The Examiner deliberately asks fast-paced follow-up questions, offers misleading backchannels, and executes harsh interruptions to test the Evaluatee's semantic resilience and adherence to turn-taking instructions.Formalizing Conversational MetricsTo quantify full-duplex performance algorithmically, these benchmarks rely on strict mathematical formalizations of speech events, utilizing Inter-Pausal Units (IPUs). An IPU is defined as a continuous stretch of speech separated by absolute silence on both sides. Historically, RL in LLMs has focused on semantic safety and instruction following. However, aligning a model for interactivity requires an entirely novel set of reward models.Rather than relying strictly on supervised fine-tuning, models like Moshi and PersonaPlex are optimized using axis-specific reward functions targeting the four canonical axes of interactivity: pause handling, turn-taking, backchanneling, and user interruption. By extracting short, overlapping audio segments from massive human conversation corpora, the models are mathematically penalized for speaking over the user inappropriately or failing to yield the floor when an explicit interruption token is detected. These RL-aligned models exhibit vast, demonstrable improvements in real-time multi-turn evaluations, setting the standard for the next generation of conversational AI.Proposed Experimentation Plan: Dual-State LLM Interruption ArchitectureTo validate a dual-state architecture where a primary "base" stream processes continuous input alongside a parallel "assessor" state generating interrupt tokens, the following experimental phases should be pursued. This approach minimizes latency by sharing the Key-Value (KV) cache while enabling predictive branching.Phase 1: Framework and Infrastructure SelectionThe primary bottleneck in a dual-state setup is GPU memory exhaustion caused by maintaining parallel branches. Standard KV caching policies (such as vLLM's PagedAttention) are optimized for completely unique prompts and fall short when managing continuous, overlapping prefixes generated by branching states. The experiment will utilize SGLang as the core inference framework. SGLang uses a specialized RadixAttention mechanism that builds a radix tree over KV-cache blocks, automatically matching and sharing memory for any prefix that multiple requests share. Because the base and the assessor share the exact identical conversational history, RadixAttention ensures that barely anything is recomputed during the prefill phase.Phase 2: Implementing the Execution BranchingUsing SGLang, the system will stream agent or user tokens into a base model context. At fixed intervals (e.g., every 5 tokens), the system will utilize SGLang's fork API primitive to create parallel copies of the prompt state.The Base Branch: Continues normal generation or listening, unaffected by the interrupt condition.The Assessor Branch: Appends a localized evaluation prompt (e.g., "Assess if STOP is required based on the constraints") to the forked state.Because both branches share the underlying radix tree, the assessor instantly accesses the entire conversation history without duplicate memory allocation, and the runtime processes the micro-divergence asynchronously.Phase 3: Advanced Cache Management and Recomputation TechniquesWhen the assessor injects its evaluation prompt, the deviation alters the sequence, potentially breaking strict prefix dependencies in less dynamic environments. To resolve boundary discrepancies caused by this splicing, the experimentation will evaluate chunk-level KV cache reuse frameworks like KVBoost.Should attention boundary errors degrade generation quality, repair algorithms will be tested:SelectiveRecompute: Targets localized boundary regions between adjacent chunks.CacheBlendRecompute: Executes a lightweight probe to identify high-deviation tokens and recalculates only those affected.Dual-Stage High Deviation (DHD): Selectively recomputes only a fraction of the KV cache when hidden states drift between the base and assessor models.Phase 4: Core Experimental TrialsTo rigorously benchmark the dual-state framework, the following experiments should be executed:Cache Hit Rate and Memory Fragmentation Evaluation: Run simulated agent-to-agent negotiation workflows through SGLang versus vLLM. Measure the total GPU VRAM consumed when the assessor branch is spawned every 5, 10, and 20 tokens. Hypothesis: SGLang’s RadixAttention will exhibit up to a 50% reduction in VRAM allocation due to automatic prefix sharing compared to non-tree-based frameworks.Assessor Branch Latency (TTFT): Measure the Time-To-First-Token (TTFT) for the assessor branch when injected mid-stream. The goal is to prove that the compute overhead of branching is negligible (sub-50ms) because the KV cache is already prefilled by the base stream.False-Positive Interruption Rate Test: Compare the accuracy of the assessor state in emitting the "STOP" token. This will evaluate whether generating a STOP signal based purely on partial context causes the model to prematurely interrupt before necessary negative constraints are delivered by the user.Phase 5: Adapting for Proprietary (Cloud-Hosted) ModelsDeploying a dual-state architecture on closed-weight models (e.g., Anthropic's Claude or OpenAI's GPT-4) presents a unique challenge, as developers cannot directly manipulate the hardware-level KV cache or use tools like SGLang's fork API. However, this architecture can be replicated via API-level Prompt Caching.Anthropic’s API, for instance, allows developers to explicitly set cache_control breakpoints within a prompt structure. To simulate the dual-state architecture via cloud APIs, the experiment will proceed as follows:State Upload: The ongoing base conversation (system prompt, tool definitions, and continuous conversational history) is uploaded to the cloud provider with strict caching metadata. The first request pays the standard prefill computational cost and latency.Assessor API Polling: To run the assessor branch, the client application makes a rapid, separate API call containing the identical cached history, appended with the new instruction: "Based on the cached history, generate 'STOP' if...".Cost and Latency Mitigation: Because the prefix perfectly matches the cloud provider's cache, the massive prefill phase is bypassed on their servers. Cache reads typically cost 90% less and drastically lower the TTFT, effectively mirroring the latency benefits of local RadixAttention.Experimental Considerations for Cloud APIs: Polling a cloud API every few tokens to act as the "assessor" risks catastrophic rate-limiting (e.g., HTTP 429 errors) and provider overload (HTTP 529 errors). For production-scale multi-agent experiments, the architecture must support a gateway router that automatically falls back to alternative hosting environments (such as AWS Bedrock or GCP Vertex AI) if the primary API stalls.By successfully mapping the local KV-cache fork to cloud-based prompt caching, the experiment will prove that the dual-state interruption framework is viable regardless of the underlying model ownership.ConclusionThe transition from turn-based LLMs to full-duplex, streaming dialogue agents represents a fundamental paradigm shift in computational linguistics and systems architecture. Enabling an LLM to accurately interpret interruptions and dynamically output its own interruption tokens requires a tight, symphonic integration of innovations across the entire technology stack.At the cognitive and algorithmic layer, Semantic VAD models, chronological thinking nodes, and confidence-conditioned wait-$k$ policies empower the LLM to contextually evaluate the exact microsecond an interruption token is warranted. This allows the system to successfully differentiate between benign human backchannels and critical, floor-claiming barge-ins. At the multi-agent architectural layer, interruptible protocols like HANDRAISER drastically slash computational overhead by permitting listening agents to halt verbose speakers the moment consensus is achievable.At the internal routing layer, cross-attention mechanisms preserve the model's latent logic during overlapping speech, preventing the catastrophic hallucination loops common in channel-fusion models. Finally, at the infrastructural hardware layer, chunked prefill algorithms, RDMA-driven disaggregated serving, and task-aware KV cache pruning ensure that the massive computational weight of context processing does not bottleneck the instantaneous generation of action tokens.As standardized benchmarks like FLEXI and Full-Duplex-Bench enforce rigorous, mathematically defined evaluation of temporal dynamics, full-duplex systems are rapidly approaching parity with human cognitive communication. The ongoing synthesis of real-time acoustic processing, durable workflow execution, and deep semantic reinforcement learning ensures that the future of LLM interaction will not merely be intelligently generated, but fluidly, instantly, and naturally negotiated.
